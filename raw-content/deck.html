The spine of this talk

Sovereignty is an engineering property

The boardroom talks about it. But sovereignty is being built — or broken — in pull requests.

Jurisdiction

Whose law can reach this workload? Follow the parent entity — not the region logo.

Control

Who holds the keys, the identity, the data?

Portability

How hard is it to leave? The off-ramp.

Transparency

Can you audit the stack? Open source, documentation, SBOMs.

All four are properties of your architecture — not of a logo.
The threat model

The CLOUD Act: a remote-access API you can't close

Applies to any US-jurisdiction entity — not to a region.
US authorities can compel disclosure of data the company "controls" — including data sitting in eu-west-1.
The region logo is not a firewall.

"No, I cannot guarantee it."

The vocabulary

SEAL — the cloud sovereignty maturity model

SEAL 0No sovereignty
us-east-1 because it was cheaper.The default.
SEAL 1Jurisdictional
EU region, GDPR.Parent still US — "GDPR compliance with a Brussels accent."
SEAL 2Data
BYOK in an EU HSM.Audit rights, contractual exit clauses.
SEAL 3Resilience
EU entity, EU operators, EU certification.The practical ceiling for most regulated workloads.
SEAL 4Full
EU-only supply chain in the critical path.Rare.
Ratings are per objective, per procurement. There is no such thing as "a SEAL-3 product".
The 8 objectives, in engineer-speak

What the Commission scores — translated

SOV-1 · 15%Strategic If the vendor's government pulled the plug, could it keep operating?
SOV-2 · 10%Legal Jurisdiction of the entity that controls the keys and the code.
SOV-3 · 10%Data & AI Cryptographic control, auditable access, EU-governed models.
SOV-4 · 15%Operational Can EU people run and migrate it? Docs, operator pool — the silent failure point: a perfect stack still stalls without skilled operators.
SOV-5 · 20% · heaviestSupply chain "Hardest to fake" — what's actually in your stack? SBOMs, provenance — a CRA legal deadline now, not a scoring nicety.
SOV-6 · 15%Technology Open, documented APIs. Open source. CNCF-aligned tooling.
SOV-7 · 10%Security ISO 27001, SecNumCloud / BSI C5, EUCS, audit rights.
SOV-8 · 5%Environmental PUE, carbon & water disclosure.
The Commission's heaviest weight = supply chain. The techies' translation: what's actually in your stack?
Off-ramp architecture

What "off-ramp ready" looks like

Portable primitives
S3-compatible storage · vanilla Postgres · OCI images · K8s with standard CNI/CSI
Keys under customer control
envelope encryption · BYOK/HYOK · KMS abstraction in the platform layer
IaC as the off-ramp
a second provider / region in the same IaC is a tested exit, not a hope
Egress
standard export formats · telemetry pipelines that survive a provider change
Named categories
Cluster API-style lifecycle mgmt · KubeVirt-pattern VMs (live migration) · service-mesh mTLS + OPA/Kyverno policy

Make it concrete

One workload, up the ladder

SEAL 0

Year 1 — us-east-1.

Cheaper. The regulatory conversation hasn't started.

SEAL 1

Year 2 — EU region.

"Almost nothing changed." The parent is still US.

SEAL 2

Year 3 — BYOK in an EU HSM.

Audit rights, 60-day exit clause. The barrier has risen. Not hypothetical — DORA Art. 28 already mandates this for financial entities.

SEAL 3

Year 4 — EU provider.

Self-managed Postgres, EU legal entity. Lambda → K8s, RDS → Postgres.

≈ €500K over 4 years ≈ 6 bps of annual volume. Affordable — if you start now. Under a regulator's deadline, it's a project, not an option.
Measure yourself

The 2×2. Be honest.

Operational Impact
Existential Risk
Independence
If you had to leave your provider, how fast could you move — without rearchitecting? → Portability, from S5
Long term, what's the full cost of that dependence — money and risk — of never being able to leave? → Portability, compounded
Others
Controlling
If access were withdrawn for certain services, what stops working — and for how long? → Control, from S5
Who can inspect, maintain, and change what runs your system — and are they subject to a foreign jurisdiction? → Transparency + Jurisdiction

Bring-home
Three things you can do
Monday morning.
No executive sign-off required.
1  BUILD it 2  TEST it 3  make it a HABIT
Action 1 · Build

Ship one off-ramp — one PR, ~1 day

Pick your most lock-in-exposed workload. Merge one concrete change:

Second region in the IaC

Exit becomes a variable, not a project.

Customer-managed keys (BYOK)

On your most sensitive store.

Wrap one proprietary SDK call

Behind an interface you own.

Deliverable: a merged PR. No new tooling, no budget.  ·  "You don't have to leave today — you just have to be able to leave."
Action 2 · Test

Run an off-ramp test — treat it like a DR drill

Snapshot + export + import into a second environment — another region, another provider, or an on-prem VM.
Quarterly. Scheduled, not improvised.
Why: Data Act Art. 25 makes switching rights contractual. Learn the gaps now — not at contract renewal in 2027.
# verify
□ export in standard formats?
□ do the keys follow?
□ app boots on vanilla Postgres
   / S3-compatible storage?
Action 3 · Habit

Add a portability gate to your review habit

Every new managed service, SDK or proprietary feature answers three lines in the PR or ADR:
$ cost to leave?
$ who holds the keys?
$ what's the off-ramp?
A team norm, not a tool. One line in the PR description is enough. This is the habit that turns sovereignty from policy into code.
